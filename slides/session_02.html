Concept 01
What is Design Thinking?
It's not about making things look good. It's a structured way to solve problems by deeply understanding the people who have them.
🧠
Starts with People
You don't guess what users need. You go find out — through research, conversations and observation.
🔁
Non-linear Process
You can go back and forth between steps. Testing might send you back to research. That's normal — not failure.
🏢
Used Everywhere
Google, Apple, IDEO, every top startup — they all use this framework to build products people love.
🎯
Solves Real Problems
Not imaginary problems. Real frustrations real users face every day — found through real research.
💡 Doctor analogy: A good doctor doesn't prescribe medicine before diagnosing the patient. A good designer doesn't open Figma before understanding the user.

Step 01 of 05
Empathise — Understand the User
Before you design anything, go find out what real people actually need. Don't assume — ask, listen, observe.
01
What you actually do
User interviews — ask people about their experience, not just what they want
Observation — watch how they use a product. What confuses them? Where do they pause?
Surveys — gather quick responses from a larger group
Competitor research — how do other apps solve the same problem?
Goal of this step
Build a real picture of who your user is. What are their goals? What frustrates them? What do they actually need vs what they say they want?
💬 Key insight: Users often say "make it faster" when what they really mean is "stop making me do this in 6 steps." Empathy helps you find the real problem.
Step 02 of 05
Define — Frame the Real Problem
Take everything you learned from research and turn it into one clear, focused problem statement. This is the most important step.
The Problem Statement Format:
[User] needs a way to [do something]
because [reason / the real insight].
Example 1: "A college student needs a way to find affordable food near campus because most delivery apps show expensive options first and don't filter by budget."

Example 2: "A working parent needs a way to track their family's medicine schedule because they often forget which medicines were given and at what time."
🎯 Why this matters: A vague problem = a vague solution. A sharp, clear problem statement guides every design decision that follows.
Steps 03 · 04 · 05
Ideate → Prototype → Test
Once you know the real problem, these three steps move you from ideas to a tested solution.
💡
03 — Ideate
Sketch 10 possible solutions — quantity first, quality second. No idea is too silly at this stage. Pick the best 1–2 to move forward with.
📐
04 — Prototype
Build the simplest version you can test. A paper sketch, a rough Figma frame — just enough that a real person can use it and give you feedback.
🧪
05 — Test
Give it to 3–5 real users. Watch what confuses them — don't explain anything. Their confusion is your next design problem to solve.
🔁 After testing: You'll almost always find new problems. Go back to Empathise or Define and start again. That's not failure — that's good design.
Real Example
Design Thinking in action
Problem: "People waste a lot of food at home." Let's walk through all 5 steps.

Step 01 — Empathise
Interview 10 people about food waste
Finding: Most people forget what's in their fridge. They discover expired food only when they're about to cook. They feel guilty but don't know how to fix it.
Key insight: It's not that people don't care — they simply can't remember what they have and when it expires.
Step 02 — Define
Write the problem statement
"Busy adults need a way to track food expiry dates at home because they forget what's in their fridge and only find out it's expired when it's too late."
Notice: The problem is specific — not just "food waste" but the exact cause: forgetting + no tracking system.
Step 03 — Ideate
Generate 10 ideas, pick the best
Ideas ranged from fridge stickers to smart labels to a full app. The team picked: a simple mobile app where users log food items and get expiry reminders.
Best idea chosen: Fridge tracker app — scan a barcode or type item name → app reminds you 2 days before expiry.
Step 04 — Prototype
3 rough screens in Figma
Screen 1: List of items in fridge with expiry dates. Screen 2: Add new item. Screen 3: Notification preview. Nothing fancy — just enough to test.
Rule: Don't spend a week on a prototype. Build the minimum version that communicates your idea to a user.
Step 05 — Test
Users tested — problems found
3 users tested the prototype. All 3 were confused by the home screen — they didn't know what to do first. The "Add item" button wasn't visible enough.
Result: Go back → redesign home screen → make "Add item" the primary action → test again. That's design thinking working exactly as it should.
Key Takeaway
"
Design Thinking starts with people, not screens.
Don't open Figma first. Understand the problem first. The best-looking screen that solves the wrong problem is still a failure.

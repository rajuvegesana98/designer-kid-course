Portfolio Guidelines

How to build a design portfolio that gets shortlisted — from what to include to where to host it.

What Recruiters Actually Look For

Recruiters spend an average of 3–5 minutes on a portfolio. They're not looking for perfection — they're looking for evidence of design thinking, communication clarity, and problem-solving maturity. Polished visuals without a story will not land interviews. A rough sketch with a clear problem statement will.

✅ Recruiters want to see

  • The problem you solved and why it mattered
  • Your research process and what you learned
  • How your design evolved through iterations
  • The outcome and what you'd change next time
  • Your specific role in team projects

❌ What won't get you hired

  • A gallery of polished screens with no context
  • Work that's 100% visual redesigns of existing apps
  • Vague role descriptions ("I designed the app")
  • No mention of users, research, or testing
  • A PDF with no link to working prototypes

How Many Projects to Include

Quality over quantity. 2–3 strong case studies will outperform a gallery of 10 shallow projects. Each project should be something you can talk about for 5 minutes from problem to outcome.

For this course: your Session 20 presentation project, your best activity work from S09 or S10, and one additional project (personal or redesign) is a solid starting portfolio.

Case Study Structure

1
OverviewProject name, your role, timeline, tools used, and a one-sentence project summary
2
The ProblemWhat user pain are you solving? Who has this problem? Why does it matter?
3
ResearchMethods used, who you talked to, key quotes, and the insight that shaped your direction
4
DefinePersona or user profile, empathy map highlights, the core problem statement (How Might We...)
5
Design ProcessSketches → wireframes → hi-fi. Show the evolution. Explain what changed and why.
6
Prototype & TestingKey test findings, specific issues users hit, and how you addressed them
7
Outcome & ReflectionWhat shipped (or what would ship), measurable improvements, and what you'd do differently

Portfolio Platforms

Behance
Best for: Visual case studies with images
Adobe's portfolio platform. Industry-standard for creative work. Excellent discoverability — recruiters actively browse Behance. Free. Long-form case studies with image grids.
Notion
Best for: Text-heavy, process-focused work
Publish as a public page with a custom domain. Easy to update. No design skills needed for the portfolio itself. Great for documenting research-heavy projects.
Framer
Best for: Impressive, interactive portfolio sites
Build a full website with animations and interactions — signals design skill by itself. Steeper learning curve. Free tier available. Hosted at a custom domain.
Figma + PDF
Best for: Email attachments and walk-throughs
Design case study slides in Figma and export as a PDF. Easier to send directly. Limit to 8–12 slides per project. Always include a Figma prototype link.

Writing Your Case Studies

Voice and tone

  • Write in first person, active voice: "I interviewed 6 users" not "6 users were interviewed."
  • Be specific: "Users abandoned the checkout at the payment screen 40% of the time" beats "users found checkout difficult."
  • Reference real constraints: "We had 2 weeks and no developer available, so I prioritised..."

Visuals

  • Always caption your screenshots — explain what the image shows and why you made that decision.
  • Show before/after pairs when you iterated based on testing.
  • Include low-fidelity work (sketches, wireframes) — it shows process, not just polish.
  • Annotate key screens with decision notes: "I moved the CTA here after 3 users missed it on the original layout."

Portfolio Checklist

  • ◆Portfolio has a home page that clearly says who you are and what you do
  • ◆Contact link or email is easy to find (not buried in a dropdown)
  • ◆2–3 case studies with problem → process → outcome structure
  • ◆Each case study includes research artifacts (interviews, empathy map, persona)
  • ◆Each case study shows wireframes AND final designs (not just final)
  • ◆At least one working Figma prototype is linked
  • ◆Your specific role is stated in every team project
  • ◆No grammar or spelling errors (have someone proofread)
  • ◆Portfolio link works and loads in under 3 seconds
  • ◆PDF version available for email applications
Update your portfolio every time you complete a significant project. A stale portfolio with old dates signals inactivity. Even a personal redesign challenge counts as recent work.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Showing only final screens: The process is more valuable than the output for junior roles.
  • Not naming the tools you used: Recruiters filter by Figma proficiency. State it explicitly.
  • Inconsistent formatting across projects: Each case study should follow the same visual structure. Use a template.
  • Making the portfolio about you instead of your users: Every sentence should connect to the user problem you were solving.
  • Password-protecting your portfolio for job applications: If recruiters can't open it in 5 seconds, they move on.

Interview Prep Guide

Questions, model answers, and frameworks to help you land your first UX/UI role.

The STAR Framework

Use STAR for every behavioural question

  • S — Situation: Set the context. What was the project? What were the constraints?
  • T — Task: What was your specific responsibility?
  • A — Action: What did YOU do? Be specific — not "we did", but "I did."
  • R — Result: What happened? Quantify if possible (usability score improved, task completion increased).

Foundational UX Questions

Common · Foundation
What is UX design and how is it different from UI design?
UX design is the process of designing the overall experience a user has with a product — covering research, information architecture, interaction design, and usability. UI design focuses specifically on the visual and interactive layer: buttons, colour, typography, layout. UX is the strategy; UI is the execution. Good UI without UX research is a beautiful product nobody can use. Good UX without UI is functional but frustrating. The best products need both.
Common · Process
Walk me through your design process.
I follow a research-driven process: (1) Discover — I start by understanding the problem through user interviews, competitive analysis, or stakeholder alignment. (2) Define — I synthesise research into personas, empathy maps, and a clear problem statement. (3) Design — I sketch flows, build wireframes, then move to high-fidelity mockups. (4) Prototype — I build clickable prototypes for user testing. (5) Test — I run moderated or unmoderated usability tests and iterate based on findings. The process isn't linear; I loop back between steps as I learn more.
Common · Research
How do you conduct user research? What methods do you use?
My method choice depends on what I need to learn. For exploratory questions (what are users' goals and mental models?), I use semi-structured interviews and contextual inquiry. For validation (does this design work?), I run usability testing — moderated sessions for complex flows, unmoderated tests for volume. I also use surveys when I need quantitative signal at scale. After collecting data, I use affinity mapping to find patterns and prioritise insights by frequency and severity.
Common · Critique
Critique an app you use every day.
I'll use Google Maps. Strengths: the map renders instantly, search is contextually smart, and the bottom drawer pattern for place details is a great use of vertical space on mobile. One issue: the "Contribute" tab buries the most useful actions behind a generic icon. A first-time user has no idea what it does. A clearer label — "Add a place" or "Share local info" — would reduce the cognitive load. Another gap: there's no way to filter real-time results by open-now status directly from the search bar without scrolling into filters. For a frequent use case, that should be one tap.
Common · Tradeoffs
Tell me about a time you had to make a design decision with incomplete data.
In my [project name] project, I had to design an onboarding flow without access to the actual users — the research timeline was compressed. I made an assumption about the primary user's technical comfort level based on secondary research (competitor reviews, Reddit discussions in that space). I documented my assumption explicitly in the design rationale. I built two low-fidelity variants — one assuming high tech comfort, one assuming low — and tested them with 4 people from a relevant community group. The simpler variant outperformed significantly, which validated my secondary research hypothesis. The key lesson: make your assumptions explicit so they can be tested.

Behavioural Questions

Behavioural · Conflict
Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder or developer on a design decision.
Situation: In a project, the product manager wanted to add a persistent promotional banner on the checkout screen because marketing data showed high CTR on similar banners elsewhere. Task: I had a usability concern — a banner at that point in the funnel would interrupt the user's intent to complete a purchase. Action: Rather than simply saying "no", I proposed running a quick moderated usability session with 5 users to test the checkout flow with and without the banner. Result: 3 out of 5 users with the banner abandoned at the checkout screen, mentioning distraction. The PM agreed to remove the banner from checkout specifically while keeping it on other screens.
Behavioural · Failure
Tell me about a design decision that didn't work out. What did you learn?
I designed a complex filter system for a product listing page based on what I thought users would want — 12 filter categories with nested options. In usability testing, users ignored most of it. They searched instead of filtering, and when they did filter, they used only 2 of the 12 categories. The lesson: I designed for edge cases and hypothetical power users instead of the 80% use case. I now start with the smallest set of features that solves the core job, and add complexity only when data supports it.
Behavioural · Collaboration
How do you work with developers?
I work hard to involve developers earlier than the handoff stage. I share wireframes and discuss feasibility before committing to hi-fi design, which prevents late-stage redesigns. At handoff, I write specs that cover spacing, colour tokens, typography, interaction states, and edge cases explicitly. I stay available during implementation for quick clarifications, and I do a QA pass before release to check that the live product matches the design intent. I've learned that developers aren't the enemy of design — they're the ones who actually ship it.

Portfolio Walkthrough Questions

Portfolio
Walk me through your most challenging project.
Structure your answer: (1) The problem and its context — who had this problem and why it mattered. (2) What made it challenging — constraints, ambiguity, conflicting stakeholder needs. (3) Your specific contributions — research methods, design decisions, iterations. (4) The outcome — what shipped, what you measured, what you'd change. Keep it to 4–5 minutes. Don't narrate every screen — tell the story of the design thinking.
Portfolio
How did you decide what problem to solve in your portfolio project?
Be ready to explain your research entry point — did you identify the problem through interviews, observation, or secondary research? Show that the problem came from users, not from your assumption that something should exist. If it was a personal project where you self-identified the problem, explain what evidence you gathered to validate that others shared the problem before designing a solution.

Questions to Ask the Interviewer

  • How does design collaborate with product and engineering on this team?
  • What does the design critique process look like here?
  • Can you tell me about a recent project where the team had to significantly pivot after user research?
  • What does success look like for a designer in this role at 3 months and 12 months?
  • What's the biggest design challenge the team is working on right now?
  • How is user research resourced — is there a dedicated researcher, or does design handle it?

Do's and Don'ts

✅ Do

  • Talk about your process, not just your outputs
  • Mention real constraints (time, resources, data)
  • Reference specific user insights that influenced your decisions
  • Acknowledge tradeoffs honestly
  • Ask thoughtful questions back
  • Have a case study you can walk through in 5 minutes

❌ Don't

  • Say "we" when you mean "I"
  • Show polished visuals without explaining the thinking
  • Claim you did everything perfectly — show how you iterated
  • Memorise answers word-for-word (sounds robotic)
  • Forget to research the company and product before the interview
  • Skip asking questions — it signals low interest
